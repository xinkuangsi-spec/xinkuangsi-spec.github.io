01 / HISTORY

Six turning points across a century of quant

From stochastic processes and portfolio selection to programmatic trading and machine learning: the tools keep changing, but the core question stays the same — where do returns come from, and who bears the risk?

1900

Bachelier brings random motion to finance

In his Théorie de la spéculation, Louis Bachelier used stochastic processes to study option pricing. It is widely regarded as the starting point of mathematical finance.

Stochastic processes
1952

Markowitz rewrites “don’t put all your eggs in one basket”

The mean–variance framework moved investing from picking individual securities to analyzing covariances between assets and total portfolio risk.

Portfolio theory
1973

Black–Scholes–Merton links pricing and hedging

Continuous-time models and dynamic replication produced a method for pricing European options — and pushed derivatives risk management toward systematization.

Derivatives pricing
1980s

Computers reach the trading desk

Electronic markets, index arbitrage, and programmatic trading expanded. Research speed, execution quality, and transaction costs became as important as the models themselves.

Programmatic trading
2000s

Data and speed become infrastructure

Finer-grained quotes, low-latency systems, and automated execution went mainstream; quant spread from an institutional specialty to a much wider set of market participants.

High frequency · Data engineering
2010s—

Machine learning joins the toolkit

Nonlinear models, alternative data, and natural language processing expanded the signal space — while making overfitting, interpretability, and data governance more critical than ever.

Machine learning
02 / MINDSET

Build research habits before hunting for a “magic strategy”

A reliable quant process doesn’t start with indicators. It starts with falsifiable hypotheses, strict data boundaries, and risks you can afford.

A / 01

Returns are a sequence, not a single number

The same total return can come from steady compounding or one lucky jump. Look at the path before the destination.

A / 02

Out-of-sample performance is the real exam

Data used for tuning can’t also prove a strategy works. Train, validation, and test periods must be strictly separated in time.

A / 03

Risk management is not a patch

Position sizing, max drawdown, liquidity, and transaction costs belong in the strategy design — not as rules bolted on after losses.

Why not compare prices directly?

Price scales differ: a $1 move from 10 to 11 means something completely different from 100 to 101. Returns standardize the change.

Simple return rₜ = (Pₜ − Pₜ₋₁) / Pₜ₋₁

  2. Compare each price with the previous one.
  3. Use the mean of returns for the center and the standard deviation for dispersion.

04 / STARTER

Lab 2: Write a minimal moving-average rule

Hold when the fast average crosses above the slow average; stay flat otherwise. This isn’t a promise of profit — it’s a clean starting point for learning the signal → position → return → cost chain.

PYTHON / PANDAS 
# df must contain a close column sorted in ascending time order
df["fast"] = df["close"].rolling(20).mean()
df["slow"] = df["close"].rolling(60).mean()

# Signals formed at the close are executed from the next period — no look-ahead
df["signal"] = (df["fast"] > df["slow"]).astype(int)
df["position"] = df["signal"].shift(1).fillna(0)

df["ret"] = df["close"].pct_change()
df["turnover"] = df["position"].diff().abs().fillna(0)
cost = 0.0005
df["strategy_ret"] = df["position"] * df["ret"] - cost * df["turnover"]

Four gates before backtesting

01

Eliminate look-ahead bias
A signal formed after the close counts from the next trading period at the earliest.

02

Deduct real costs
Commissions, spreads, and slippage eat the edge of frequently traded strategies.

03

Reserve an out-of-sample window
The final stretch of history stays out of tuning — it’s for the final check only.

04

Stress-test extremes
Watch max drawdown, losing streaks, and liquidity — not just average return.

07 / FAQ

Quant trading: six questions beginners ask most

Each answer leads with a one-sentence conclusion, then the key details — quick to scan, easy to cite.

What is quantitative trading?

Quantitative trading uses mathematical models and computer programs to support investment decisions. It turns “what to buy, when, and how much” into explicit rules, then tests them on historical data. Unlike discretionary trading, its edge lies in discipline, repeatability, and measurable risk — not magic that predicts prices.

Where did quantitative finance begin?

The widely accepted starting point is Louis Bachelier’s 1900 doctoral thesis, Théorie de la spéculation, the first work to study option pricing with stochastic processes. Markowitz’s portfolio theory (1952) and the Black–Scholes–Merton option pricing model (1973) then turned finance into computable mathematics, step by step.

Can a complete beginner learn quantitative trading? What background is needed?

Yes — but you’ll need three foundations: Python programming (data work), probability and statistics (to understand volatility and risk), and finance basics (returns, portfolios, derivatives). This site’s four-week roadmap follows exactly that order: compute returns first, then risk and portfolios, then your first moving-average strategy, and finally out-of-sample validation.

Does quantitative trading always make money?

No. Any strategy can lose money, and past backtests don’t predict future performance. The real value of quant is making risk explicit: max drawdown, transaction costs, overfitting, and out-of-sample failure all matter more than average return. The “four gates before backtesting” on this page — no look-ahead, real costs, an out-of-sample reserve, extreme scenarios — are exactly where beginners get hurt most.

What is mean–variance portfolio theory?

Harry Markowitz’s 1952 framework: investors shouldn’t look only at an asset’s expected return in isolation, but at covariances between assets, using diversification to lower total portfolio risk. It turned “don’t put all your eggs in one basket” into a computable optimization problem — the beginning of modern portfolio theory.

What are the most common backtesting mistakes?

Three: ① look-ahead bias — using data that didn’t exist yet at decision time; ② overfitting — parameters that only work on historical data; ③ ignoring costs — commissions and slippage devour the profits of high-turnover strategies. The fixes: lag signals by one period, deduct real costs, and hold out an out-of-sample window for the final check only.
